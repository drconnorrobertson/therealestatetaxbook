Short-Term Rental Tax Rules: From Guest Stays to Loss Review

A practical sequence for reviewing classification, participation and usable deductions

Published June 29, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026 · AE Tax Advisors

When a property is marketed as a short-term rental, its listing category does not settle its federal loss treatment. Start with the actual customer-use records, then evaluate the owner's participation and the return's deduction limits. The phrase “STR tax loophole” can obscure those separate decisions.

This guide provides an original review sequence and fictional stay-length example. It does not calculate an actual taxpayer's refund, establish a property's depreciation life or promise that a loss offsets salary.

First, classify the activity using its actual stays

Treasury Regulation 1.469-1T(e)(3) provides exceptions to rental-activity treatment for Section 469. One applies when average customer use is seven days or less. Another addresses averages of 30 days or less with significant personal services; there are additional exceptions. An average above seven days therefore does not resolve every case. This classification is specific to passive-activity rules; it does not by itself decide reporting forms, self-employment tax or depreciation treatment.

A fictional single-property stay calculation

Assume one class of property and distinct, completed customer-use periods that your preparer has confirmed belong in the calculation. Start with 20 stays of three days: 20 × 3 = 60 days, and 60 ÷ 20 = a three-day average. Now add four stays of 30 days each: (60 + 120) ÷ (20 + 4) = 7.5 days. The longer stays change the annual result even though most bookings remain short.

Reconcile the booking export to contracts, extensions and cancellations. Keep personal-use and vacant days identifiable rather than treating every calendar day as a customer-use day. For multiple properties, classes of property or grouping questions, obtain a property-specific calculation rather than copying this single-property illustration.

Second, identify the participation test and its evidence

Treasury Regulation 1.469-5T lists seven material-participation tests. Test 1 requires more than 500 hours. Test 2 concerns substantially all participation. Test 3 requires more than 100 hours and at least as much participation as any other individual, including a nonowner. Test 7 is a separate facts-and-circumstances test with restrictions; it is not the more-than-100-hours comparison test.

A log is useful, but the total needs a defensible explanation. Distinguish operational work from investor monitoring, which generally does not count unless the owner is directly involved in daily management or operations. A spouse's participation can count. The rules permit reasonable proof without requiring contemporaneous daily logs; a retrospective round-number estimate is a poor substitute for supporting records. See IRS Publication 925 and the participation-records companion guide.

Build a review worksheet before choosing a conclusion

  • Activity: property address, ownership, year and any grouping treatment requiring review.
  • Customer use: completed periods, total days, calculated average and unresolved booking differences.
  • Owner work: date, person, task, duration and supporting calendar entry, message or invoice.
  • Other people: manager, cleaner and contractor responsibilities; identify missing individual-hour evidence if relying on a comparison test.
  • Conclusion: the selected test, excluded time, open questions and who will resolve them before filing.

For example, if an owner's worksheet shows 140 hours but the manager's hours are unknown, write “comparison unresolved.” Do not replace missing evidence with an assumption that owning the property means the owner did the most work. The worksheet should make that gap visible to the reviewer.

Third, model the amount that can actually be used

A potential depreciation deduction, a property loss and a current return deduction are different numbers. Publication 925 explains basis, at-risk and passive-activity limitations. The Form 461 instructions address excess business losses after applicable basis, at-risk and passive limits; use the instructions for the tax year being filed.

Do not subtract a projected property loss from gross wages and call the result taxable income. Prepare the complete return comparison, including other income, deductions, suspended amounts and state treatment. Label any proposed cost-segregation allocation as unverified until its asset classification, eligibility and placed-in-service facts have been reviewed. The deduction-versus-usable-loss guide helps organize that separate analysis.

Keep the reporting and services question separate

IRS Publication 527 distinguishes basic rental services from substantial services provided primarily for a tenant's convenience. Services can affect Schedule C reporting and self-employment tax. Nonpassive treatment alone does not settle those questions. Give the preparer the actual guest-service agreement and service schedule rather than just the platform's property category.

Bring the unresolved facts to a property review

Prepare the booking reconciliation, participation worksheet, ownership documents, depreciation schedule and prior suspended-loss records together. Ask for separate conclusions on classification, participation, reporting and current loss usability. If an input is missing, record a follow-up responsibility rather than presenting a projected tax benefit as established.
