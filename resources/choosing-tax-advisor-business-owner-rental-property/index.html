Choosing a Tax Advisor When You Own a Business and Rental Property

Compare advisory proposals across your operating business, rental properties and owner return. Define deliverables, implementation duties, records and fees before hiring.

Published November 6, 2026 · By AE Tax Advisors

Start with the decision you are hiring someone to resolve

A business owner with rental property may need more than annual return preparation. The immediate decision could be buying a building, ordering a cost segregation study, reviewing a related-party lease, correcting depreciation, or selling an operating company while keeping its premises. Name that decision before comparing firms. A proposal for preparing last year’s returns is different from a proposal to model a purchase and coordinate its implementation.

Write down the event, the affected entities, the date by which a decision is needed, and the records already available. Include the personal return because an entity-level deduction does not establish its owner-level tax effect. This one-page outline lets competing advisers respond to the same facts. It also reduces the chance that the engagement starts with a broad strategy list while the actual transaction remains unaddressed.

Compare the complete ownership picture

Ask how the adviser will connect the operating business, the property-owning entity and the individual owners. Identify whether other partners or family members have interests in either business. Include existing leases, financing and planned changes in use. Do not assume an LLC name tells the adviser its federal tax classification or that common ownership makes every payment or loss receive the same treatment.

For example, a profitable dental practice may operate in a building owned through a separate entity and also have unrelated rental investments. A useful scope distinguishes the practice’s operations, rent and property expenses, the separate rentals, and the owner’s return. The goal is a coordinated fact pattern, not an assumption that a depreciation study automatically offsets practice profit. Passive-activity, basis, at-risk and other applicable limits need individual review.

Separate the written plan from the work needed to implement it

Ask for deliverables rather than a promise to “optimize taxes.” A written analysis might include assumptions, alternatives, a projection, evidence requirements, deadlines and responsible people. Implementation can involve bookkeeping changes, payroll work, an engineering study, elections, an accounting-method change, returns or amendments. Each item should have a named owner and a clear completion standard.

If an outside firm prepares the returns, ask what it will receive and whether the adviser will answer its implementation questions. A study delivered as a PDF is not the same as completed reporting on a tax return. Form 3115 is used to request a change in accounting method; it is not a universal substitute for an amended return. Ask the professional to identify the appropriate procedure for the specific depreciation issue instead of choosing a filing route in advance.

Use the same proposal checklist for each firm

Compare the initial engagement and the following year separately. Confirm which entities, properties, tax years and jurisdictions are included. Ask whether the fee includes personal and business returns, extensions, amendment work, study preparation, payroll coordination, bookkeeping cleanup or notices. If something is separately priced, request the basis for that fee and the event that triggers additional work.

Also ask about the service period, renewal requirements, meeting cadence, response expectations, document portal and the process for changes in scope. A lower quoted price may cover less work; a higher price does not prove a better result. Ask how a refund or cancellation provision works in the actual agreement. Keep proposed savings separate from fees and compare the assumptions behind every projection.

Verify credentials and the boundaries of representation

The IRS explains that tax preparers have different qualifications and representation rights. Check the named professional’s credentials with the appropriate licensing body or IRS directory where applicable, rather than relying only on a firm’s brand. Ask who performs the analysis, who signs a return, who reviews the work and who would represent you if a tax authority asks questions.

Credential checks do not replace a scope discussion. A tax specialist may coordinate with, rather than replace, an attorney, engineer, lender or bookkeeper. For a property acquisition or business sale, ask which legal or financing questions require those other professionals. Be cautious about guaranteed savings, guaranteed audit outcomes or recommendations made before the adviser has reviewed the material facts.

Prepare a useful first conversation without sending a full document dump

Bring a summary of the business, property types, entity ownership, approximate complexity and the decision timeline. Identify whether prior returns, depreciation schedules, closing statements, leases and carryforward records are available. You do not need to send account numbers or tax returns through an ordinary booking form to establish whether a firm handles the work.

After the engagement and secure document process are confirmed, provide the records the assigned professional requests. Keep a list of missing items and who will obtain each one. If you change advisers, request a handoff that preserves depreciation history, relevant elections and unresolved questions. A new professional should not have to infer those facts from a current profit-and-loss report alone.

Decide when a planning engagement is worth scoping

A coordinated engagement may be useful when a material purchase, improvement, sale, ownership change or filing correction affects both your business and your real estate. It can also be appropriate when different preparers are working with inconsistent assumptions. If the need is routine compliance and records are straightforward, a narrower preparation engagement may be sufficient. The decision should follow complexity and required work, not an income label alone.
