Inherited Rental Property: Hire a Tax Review Before Listing or Selling

An inherited rental can arrive with tenants, debt and incomplete records. Before listing it or treating the estimated proceeds as spendable cash, ask a tax adviser to build a property-specific basis and reporting file. This review is especially useful when you also own a business, have other rentals or share the inherited property with family members.

Identify what was inherited and who can act

Start with the deed, estate or trust documents, date of death and contact information for the personal representative or trustee. Clarify whether you inherited the property directly, an interest in an entity that owns it, or a beneficial interest that has not yet been distributed. Those situations should not be treated as interchangeable. Ask the estate attorney to resolve authority and title questions.

The engagement should identify whose returns the tax adviser will handle. The decedent, estate or trust, and beneficiaries may have separate reporting responsibilities. IRS Publication 559 explains the broader survivor and estate reporting framework. Do not assume that hiring someone for your personal return includes the estate's filings.

Establish the valuation evidence before calculating gain

Inherited-property basis generally depends on the applicable estate valuation rules, with exceptions. Obtain a defensible valuation for the relevant date rather than relying only on the current asking price, a county assessment or the deceased owner's original purchase amount. Ask whether an estate tax return, alternate valuation election or basis statement affects the number your preparer must use.

IRS Publication 551 describes inherited-property basis and important qualifications. A basis adjustment can be downward as well as upward. The adviser should identify the inherited interest and the support for its basis, not simply write a new number into a depreciation schedule. Joint ownership and ownership through an entity require their own analysis.

Reconstruct operations after the death

Collect rent statements, management reports, repair invoices, insurance, taxes and mortgage records for the period after death. Separate receipts and expenses by the owner or taxpayer responsible for reporting them. If the property changed use, was vacant for renovations or was partly used by a beneficiary, document dates and facts rather than assuming the historical rental treatment continued unchanged.

Ask for an opening asset schedule showing how the rental building, land and other assets are treated. Keep the valuation and allocation support with that schedule. IRS Publication 527 explains rental depreciation and operating records. The review should address your post-inheritance facts rather than automatically continuing every number from the prior owner's return.

Commission a sale model that reconciles to cash

Provide a realistic price range, estimated closing expenses, property debt, improvements after inheritance and the expected closing date. Ask for separate calculations of taxable gain, potential tax and net cash after debt payoff. Debt payoff and taxable gain are different concepts; a large loan balance does not establish that there will be little taxable income.

Request a comparison of selling now and continuing to rent only if both are actual options. State the assumptions explicitly, including ownership, depreciation, applicable state taxes and the timing of payments. If multiple beneficiaries are involved, ask how each person's ownership and reporting will be reflected. One family-wide estimate is not a substitute for taxpayer-specific work.

Agree on the completed work product

  • A document list showing what was reviewed and what remains missing.
  • A supported basis schedule and explanation of material assumptions.
  • A record of post-death rental operations and depreciation treatment.
  • A sale estimate separating taxable amounts from cash proceeds.
  • A list of returns and estimated-payment work included in the engagement.
  • A handoff plan between the estate attorney, fiduciary and tax preparer.

Ask which unanswered questions must be resolved before signing a sale contract and which can be completed before filing. Avoid promising a closing timeline based on records that have not yet been obtained. This review does not replace appraisal, legal or investment advice.
